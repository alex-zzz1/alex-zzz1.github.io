Ottawa to Toronto. The trip from my hometown to my school, my court, my playground. The first time I made this trip, it took six hours: my mother drove carefully, terrified of making any mistake and bringing harm to her baby as she brought him to see the big city. The first long car ride in my conscious memory, it felt like I was sitting in the silver Mazda Six for...six hours. But it seemed a long time back then! A wide-eyed child, I stared at every rock feature and body of water that flew by our silver spaceship as I pestered my mom with “are we there yet?” The next few, it took around five: the trip became quite regular as tournaments happened often and happened always in the big city. My carpooling teammates and I would play trivia games in minivans and hatchbacks, exhausting every five-lettered country we knew to kill time. We couldn’t wait as our red-or-black-or-white chariots charged towards the coming battle (The colour depended on whose parents were piloting our war-bound chariot. My favourite was my mom’s environmentally friendly red one). Whimsy and nervousness mixed around in my gut. Getting dropped off to begin my first year only took four hours thanks to my father’s canonically great driving; mastery over the art of SUV. As though fast-forwarded, the last few days of my adolescent life and this trip felt short, with barely enough time to notice the Southern Ontario pre-autumn scenery. Waves of nervousness crashed over overwhelmed whimsy, sloshing with the blue-grey paddy wagon’s oscillations as we marched toward my four-year sentence. What if I couldn’t make friends? What if Physics was too hard? What if I can’t make the team? Recently, I drove the whole way for the first time. Taking three hours and some minutes, I blazed through the freeway in my best friend’s rental car, catching up on our transformative, treacherous, trivial university lives as he sat hungover in the passenger seat. I was escaping an arduous semester that hurt terribly yet was over in an instant, followed by three fleeting days of play and parties that did not appease my disheartenment. No longer a child, I drove the getaway vehicle with my dad’s skill and my mom’s sense for speed control, fleeing the crime scene of academics, athletics, and adult life to the cradle of home. The voyage flew by as we laughed away (and because the getaway driver was speeding), reckless young adults. Trips between the two cities come in pairs of two. As of late, I often took green commercial buses. For every sprint I took in these blocky green shoes toward the big city and my responsibilities, same too there is another away from it, back to my mother and my two childhood rooms. Too often now, they blend together, feeling like a fraction of the length they used to. Compared to when I was little, I feel twofold less nervous when I begin the races and twofold less relieved when I finish them. Once, a thought wandered to me: I wonder when the last one of these trips will be? On that day, which city will be the one I land in? How will I feel when I find out the answer is Toronto?
